New York City grew while its daily water demand declined. This temporal study asks how a long historical series can make that counterintuitive relationship visible without turning it into a conventional dashboard.

00 / CONTEXT

Attempt

I used temporal structure to compare total water consumption, per-capita demand, and population across forty-six years. The goal was to show that urban growth and resource demand do not always move in the same direction.

Method

The page follows the tutorial sequence: load an external CSV, parse dates and numbers, bind data to SVG, build time and linear scales, add axes, tooltips, transitions, and coordinated views.

Dataset

Water Consumption in the City of New York, published through NYC Open Data by the Department of Environmental Protection. The included CSV is a static 1979–2024 snapshot.

Limitation

Annual citywide averages hide seasonal peaks, borough differences, building types, leakage, pricing, and unequal household access. The chart describes a system-level trend, not individual behavior.

05 / READING

The temporal argument

Time reveals decoupling. A single year only shows a quantity. The full sequence reveals a structural divergence: population grows, yet total demand falls.

Scale changes interpretation. Total consumption describes infrastructure demand; per-capita consumption reframes the same system as average demand per resident.

Transitions preserve continuity. The animated metric switch keeps the same years in place, allowing the viewer to compare measures without losing temporal orientation.
